\section{Background of the Study}
\hs Consumer price inflation is an important economic indicator because it reflects changes in the prices of goods and services purchased by households. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) is commonly used to measure these changes by comparing the cost of a representative basket of consumer goods and services over time. In Cambodia, the National Institute of Statistics (NIS) is the official institution responsible for producing and publishing the Consumer Price Index and related inflation statistics. NIS publishes CPI information on a monthly basis, providing an important measure of price developments in the Cambodian economy.

\hs Although the official CPI provides a standardized and reliable measure of inflation, its monthly publication creates a time lag between the period in which price changes occur and the time when the corresponding official information becomes available. This creates a challenge for timely monitoring of current price conditions. When consumer prices change rapidly, particularly for frequently purchased products such as food and other essential goods, the latest officially published CPI may describe price conditions from an earlier period rather than the current situation.

\hs The increasing availability of online retail data provides an opportunity to reduce this information gap. Many retailers and online shopping platforms publish product prices electronically, allowing prices to be observed at a much higher frequency than traditional monthly statistical collection. Instead of waiting for a monthly CPI release, online prices can potentially be collected every day and used to monitor changes in consumer prices continuously.

\hs However, collecting online prices alone does not produce a measure that can be directly compared with the official CPI. Products must first be identified and organized into appropriate expenditure categories, price observations must be cleaned and standardized, and changes in individual product prices must be aggregated into meaningful price indices. A consistent methodology is therefore required to transform high-frequency retail price observations into an indicator of overall consumer price movements.

\hs In this study, daily online retail prices collected from selected Cambodian sources are organized according to the relevant CPI expenditure categories. The Jevons price index is applied at the elementary aggregation level to measure price changes among comparable products, while a Laspeyres-type weighted aggregation is applied to combine the resulting indices using expenditure weights and obtain the CPI measure at a higher level. The resulting daily price information provides a higher-frequency view of consumer price movements than the official monthly CPI.

\hs The study further uses the high-frequency price information to forecast inflation ahead of the official monthly CPI release. \texttt{RidgeCV} is employed as the forecasting model because it provides a regularized regression approach that can handle multiple potentially correlated explanatory variables while selecting the regularization parameter through cross-validation.

\hs Therefore, this study focuses on using automatically collected online retail prices to reduce the information gap created by the publication lag of the official NIS CPI. By combining automated data collection, structured data processing, price-index calculation, and inflation forecasting, the study aims to provide an earlier indication of inflation conditions in Cambodia.


\section{Problem Statement}
\hs The official Consumer Price Index (CPI) published by the National Institute of Statistics (NIS) is an important source of information for measuring inflation in Cambodia. However, the official CPI is published on a monthly basis and becomes available only after the corresponding reference period. As a result, there is a time lag between the actual movement of consumer prices and the availability of official inflation information.

\hs This publication lag creates a limitation for timely inflation monitoring. Consumer prices do not remain constant throughout a month. Prices of food, household products, fuel, and other frequently purchased goods can change because of changes in supply, demand, promotions, seasonal conditions, transportation costs, and other market factors. When such changes occur, users of official statistics may have to wait for the next official CPI release before obtaining a standardized measure of overall price movements.

\hs The problem is therefore not the absence of consumer price information. Retailers and online shopping platforms already publish prices for many products on a frequent basis. The problem is that these high-frequency price observations are not automatically collected and transformed into a systematic price index that can provide an earlier indication of inflation before the official NIS CPI is released.

\hs Furthermore, raw online prices cannot be directly used as an alternative to the official CPI. Products may differ across retailers, product descriptions may be inconsistent, prices may change temporarily because of promotions, and some observations may be missing or duplicated. Therefore, an automated system is required to continuously collect, organize, validate, and process the available online price data. An appropriate index methodology is also required to aggregate individual price changes into a consistent measure of consumer price movements.

\hs This study therefore addresses the following central problem:

\begin{center}
\textbf{How can the time lag in the availability of official NIS CPI information be reduced by using high-frequency online retail price data to produce an earlier indication of consumer price inflation in Cambodia?}
\end{center}

\hs To address this problem, the study develops an automated system that collects online retail prices on a daily basis, stores and processes the structured price data, applies the Jevons and Laspeyres index approaches to calculate price indices, and uses \texttt{RidgeCV} to forecast inflation ahead of the official monthly CPI release.


\section{Project Goal and Research Objectives}
\subsection{Project Goal}
\hs The goal of this project is to close the gap between how quickly consumer prices actually change in Cambodia and how slowly the official statistical system currently measures them, by using automated, daily online price data to support faster inflation monitoring.

\subsection{General Objective}
\hs To achieve this goal, the project designs and builds an end-to-end system that collects, stores, processes, and analyzes online consumer price data, and uses it to estimate consumer price inflation on a daily basis.

\subsection{Specific Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Build an automated data collection and monitoring system:} Set up a pipeline that continuously collects and stores price data from online retail sources, and monitors it for completeness and quality.
    \item \textbf{Calculate the price index and forecast inflation:} Apply an established economic formula to compute daily price relatives, and use \texttt{RidgeCV}-based modeling to forecast inflation ahead of the official monthly release.
    \item \textbf{Build a decision-support dashboard:} Develop an interactive dashboard that presents daily CPI results and forecasted inflation for easy monitoring.
\end{enumerate}



\section{Scope and Limitations}
\subsection{Scope of the Study}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Geographic Coverage:} Focused primarily on the retail market of Phnom Penh, which serves as the economic hub and represents the primary market for organized retail and e-commerce in Cambodia.
    \item \textbf{Taxonomy Breadth:} Full coverage across all 12 United Nations COICOP divisions (Divisions 01 through 12).
    \item \textbf{Temporal Frequency:} Daily price quote extraction, daily index calculation, and historical time-series backfilling anchored to August 18, 2026 ($CPI = 100.00$).
    \item \textbf{Bilingual and Currency Handling:} Dual-currency pricing (USD and KHR) unified into Cambodian Riel via daily official MEF exchange rates.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations of the Study}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Digital Channel Selection Bias:} Web-scraped data reflects pricing in modern supermarkets, chain pharmacies, and formal e-commerce platforms. Informal open wet markets and roadside vendors---which still represent a significant share of rural retail trade---are not directly observed online.
    \item \textbf{Retail Website Stability:} Scraping pipelines are dependent on external website uptime and DOM structures. Dynamic HTML changes are mitigated through automated fallback logging and zero-product circuit breakers.
    \item \textbf{Base Period Anchor:} Index calculations are computed relative to the base period of August 18, 2026, establishing a high-frequency benchmark for ongoing price dynamics.
\end{itemize}

\section{Significance and Contributions of the Study}
\hs This research provides significant practical, institutional, and academic contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{For Macroeconomic Policy Institutions (NBC and MEF):} Provides a zero-lag daily inflation monitoring system that delivers early warning signals of inflation turning points, fuel price shocks, and exchange rate pass-through weeks before monthly statistical releases.
    \item \textbf{For National Statistical Authorities (NIS):} Establishes a replicable data engineering and machine learning framework for integrating alternative digital big data into official national statistical compilation.
    \item \textbf{For Academic Applied Data Science:} Demonstrates the novel fusion of modern cloud Medallion data engineering, dense vector similarity, Large Language Model reasoning pools, and micro-econometric index theory in a developing Southeast Asian economic context.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chapter II (Literature Review):} Reviews the academic, central banking, and international statistical literature on high-frequency web scraping, natural language processing for product classification, axiomatic index number theory, and real-time inflation nowcasting.
    \item \textbf{Chapter III (Methodology):} Details the end-to-end Medallion data engineering architecture, the 25 market data sources, data cleaning and unit standardization procedures, Store Priority routing and Gemini AI classification for new products with domain trap guardrails, axiomatic Jevons aggregation, and the high-frequency inflation nowcasting model.
